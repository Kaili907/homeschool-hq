## PART 1: READING COMPREHENSION (~30 min)

### Passage A — nonfiction (read, then answer 1–10)

**The Bridge That Joined a State**

For most of Michigan's history, the state was really two states. The Upper and Lower Peninsulas, separated by the Straits of Mackinac, might as well have been different countries. Travelers who wanted to cross the five miles of water between them waited for ferries — sometimes for hours, and during hunting season, sometimes for a full day, in lines of cars that stretched for miles. In winter, ice could stop the boats entirely. Business owners in the Upper Peninsula watched customers and opportunities stay south. Politicians had talked about a bridge since the 1880s, but the Straits seemed to forbid one: the water ran deep, the winds howled through the gap between two Great Lakes, and winter ice heaved and ground against anything humans put in its way.

Engineers were not sure such a bridge could be built. Some proposals were dismissed as fantasies; one plan even suggested a floating tunnel. The project finally moved forward in the early 1950s, when the state created an authority to finance the bridge with bonds — money borrowed from investors, to be repaid with tolls — rather than waiting for tax dollars that never seemed to arrive. The designer, David Steinman, had spent his career building suspension bridges, and he drew up a structure meant not merely to survive the Straits but to shrug them off. Its roadway grating was designed to let fierce winds pass through instead of pushing against a solid deck. Its towers were set on foundations sunk deep into the lakebed.

Construction began in 1954 and took three and a half years. Thousands of workers labored through brutal winters, and five of them lost their lives building it. When the Mackinac Bridge opened to traffic in 1957, it stretched nearly five miles from shore to shore, with a main span of 3,800 feet suspended between its towers — an engineering feat that transformed a day-long wait into a ten-minute drive.

The bridge changed more than travel times. The two peninsulas, long strangers, became neighbors. Tourism in the Upper Peninsula grew as downstate families discovered how easily they could now reach its forests and waterfalls. And the "Mighty Mac" itself became the state's most recognizable landmark — proof, Michiganders like to say, that the thing everyone calls impossible is sometimes just waiting for someone stubborn enough to design it properly.

**Questions (short answers or circle):**
1. What is the central idea of the passage?
2. What specific problem did travelers face before the bridge? Give two details from the text.
3. Why did some engineers doubt the bridge could be built? Name two reasons the passage gives.
4. How was the bridge paid for, and why was that approach chosen?
5. In paragraph 2, what does the phrase "shrug them off" suggest about Steinman's design goals?
6. What design feature dealt with the Straits' winds, and how did it work?
7. What is the author's purpose in the final paragraph?
8. How is the passage organized: (a) comparison, (b) problem and solution told chronologically, (c) cause listed after effect, (d) pure description? Briefly defend your choice.
9. Identify one sentence in the passage that is opinion rather than fact, and explain how you know.
10. In the last sentence, what does the author imply about how Michiganders view the bridge beyond its practical use?

### Passage B — fiction (read, then answer 11–15)

Mara stood at the edge of the mat, tugging at the cuff of her sleeve the way she always did before a match she expected to lose. Across from her, the girl from Riverside rolled her shoulders like someone who had never once considered losing anything. The referee raised his hand. Mara's coach said something from the corner — she couldn't hear it over the blood in her ears, but she knew what it was, because he only ever said one thing: *Position before submission. Slow is smooth.* She stopped tugging her sleeve. The whistle blew, and for the first two seconds, Mara did nothing at all — she just watched the other girl rush in, exactly the way film said she would.

11. What can you infer about how Mara feels at the start of the scene? Cite the detail that shows it.
12. What does the contrast between Mara and the Riverside girl's body language accomplish?
13. What does Mara's decision to "do nothing at all" for two seconds reveal about her? What earlier detail supports this?
14. What is the effect of italicizing the coach's saying rather than putting it in quotation marks?
15. Based on the final sentence, what do you predict happens next, and what in the text supports your prediction?

### Reading Key (score judgment answers generously if reasoned from the text)
1. The Mackinac Bridge overcame doubts and physical obstacles to unite Michigan's two peninsulas, changing the state. 2. Hours-to-day-long ferry waits, miles-long car lines, ice stopping boats. 3. Deep water, severe winds, grinding ice (any two). 4. Bonds repaid by tolls; tax money never materialized. 5. He aimed for a design that would master the conditions easily, not just endure them. 6. Open roadway grating lets wind pass through the deck instead of pushing on it. 7. To show the bridge's larger meaning/legacy beyond transportation. 8. (b) — problem established, then solution unfolds in time order. 9. e.g., "the state's most recognizable landmark" or the closing "proof..." claim — evaluative claims, not verifiable in the text. 10. It's a symbol of stubborn achievement/state identity. 11. Nervous/expects to lose — sleeve-tugging habit "before a match she expected to lose." 12. Highlights the confidence gap; sets up underdog stakes. 13. Discipline/strategy over panic — she studied film and follows her coach's patience principle; supported by her stopping the sleeve-tugging. 14. Presents it as internalized memory/mantra rather than spoken words. 15. Reasonable: she counters the rush because she prepared for it; supported by "exactly the way film said she would."

**Reading bands (15 questions):** 13–15 = strong; curriculum goes straight to challenging literature and analysis. 10–12 = solid with gaps; note whether misses cluster in inference (11–15 type) or nonfiction analysis (7–10 type) and weight the curriculum there. Below 10 = comprehension becomes a daily explicit focus before heavy literature.

## PART 2: GRAMMAR, USAGE & MECHANICS (15 items, ~20 min)

Fix, choose, or identify as directed. Rewrites must be written out.

1. Identify the problem and fix it: *Because the storm knocked out the power for three days.*
2. Fix this sentence: *The trail was muddy we hiked it anyway.*
3. Choose: *The box of old photographs (was / were) in the attic.*
4. Choose: *Neither of the sisters (want / wants) to go first.*
5. Choose: *The coaches gave the trophy to Marcus and (I / me).*
6. This sentence is unclear — rewrite it so the meaning is certain: *When Dana talked to her sister, she was upset.*
7. Choose (the room belongs to many girls): *The (girls / girl's / girls') locker room was crowded.*
8. Choose: *The team lost (its / it's) first game.*
9. Choose: *The new rule will (affect / effect) everyone.*
10. Choose: *(There / Their / They're) going to regret leaving (there / their / they're) jackets over (there / their / they're).*
11. Add the missing punctuation: *After we finished dinner we walked to the park.*
12. Fix the faulty parallelism: *She likes swimming, hiking, and to ride bikes.*
13. Fix the tense problem: *He opened the door and sees the mess.*
14. Fix the modifier problem: *Running to catch the bus, my backpack fell open.*
15. Fix the capitalization: *We visited lake Michigan last summer.*

### Grammar Key
1. Fragment — attach it: "Because the storm knocked out the power for three days, we ate by candlelight" (any complete fix). 2. Run-on — "The trail was muddy, but we hiked it anyway" (or semicolon/period). 3. was. 4. wants. 5. me. 6. Any rewrite that fixes the ambiguous *she*: "Dana was upset when she talked to her sister" / "Dana's sister was upset when they talked." 7. girls'. 8. its. 9. affect. 10. They're / their / there. 11. Comma after "dinner." 12. "...swimming, hiking, and riding bikes." 13. "...and saw the mess." 14. Any fix giving the runner a subject: "Running to catch the bus, I let my backpack fall open" / "As I ran to catch the bus, my backpack fell open." 15. Lake Michigan.

**Grammar bands:** 13–15 = mechanics are solid; English time goes to style, structure, and analysis, with grammar handled only in editing. 9–12 = targeted mini-lessons on the missed categories, woven into weekly writing. Below 9 = a 4–6 week grammar bootcamp opens the year — for the senior, run it inside her essay revisions, not as separate worksheets (no time for both).

## PART 3: TIMED ESSAY (45 minutes, separate sitting)

**Prompt (both girls):** *Should high schools require every student to learn a practical trade skill — like welding, coding, cooking, or auto repair — before graduating? Take a position and argue it. Use specific reasons and examples.*

Rules: 45 minutes total, planning on scratch paper encouraged, handwritten or typed, no help, no looking things up. Turn in the planning page too — it's diagnostic gold.

**What I'll score it on (1–4 each):** clear arguable thesis · organization (intro, body paragraphs that each do one job, conclusion) · development (specific reasons and examples, not repeated assertions) · sentence control and variety · conventions.

**Grade-level expectations:** Sophomore on-track = solid five-ish paragraph structure, real examples, mostly clean mechanics. Senior on-track = everything the sophomore has, plus acknowledging and answering the other side, and some deliberate style. The gap between what each produces and these bars becomes her writing curriculum.

**Senior only — Part 3B (30 min, ungraded, different day):** *Describe a time you failed at something that mattered to you, and what you did after.* This is a college-application-essay diagnostic — it shows me her narrative voice, not her five-paragraph training. Tell her it's ungraded and to write like herself.
