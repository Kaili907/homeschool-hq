## The Fence That Learned to Bend (`g4-text-01`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 1

The snow fence along the north field had blown down every winter for as long as anyone could remember, and every spring Grandpa Elias rebuilt it stronger.

He used heavier posts one year. The wind took them. He used deeper post holes the next year. The wind took those too, tearing the slats apart and scattering them into the ditch like spilled matchsticks.

June was eleven the winter she started keeping notes. She wrote down the direction of the storms, which was almost always northwest, and where the fence broke first, which was almost always the same forty-foot stretch near the low spot. She wrote down something else: the fence never broke where the old lilac hedge stood beside it.

"The hedge holds it," she told her grandfather.

"The hedge is a windbreak," he said. "A fence is not a hedge."

"No," June said. "The hedge moves."

He stopped stacking slats and looked at her.

That spring they rebuilt the forty-foot stretch differently. They set the posts no deeper than before, but they left the slats loose at one end, so the whole panel could give a little and swing back. Grandpa Elias called it sloppy work three separate times while he was doing it.

The first storm came in November, hard out of the northwest. June stood at the kitchen window and watched the loose section shudder, lean, and rock back upright. In the morning it was still standing. The tight, strong, properly built section forty feet to the east lay flat in the snow.

Grandpa Elias walked the fence line twice without saying anything. Then he came in, poured coffee, and set his hat on the table.

"Fifty years," he said, "I have been building it to win."

June waited.

"A thing that will not move," he said, "is just waiting to break."

## What a Storm Leaves Behind (`g4-text-02`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 1

When a severe thunderstorm passes over a town, the damage people notice first is rarely the damage that matters most.

Broken branches and scattered trash are obvious, and they are cleaned up within a day or two. Meanwhile, three slower effects begin working underneath the visible mess.

The first is saturated soil. Heavy rain fills the spaces between soil particles until the ground can hold no more water. Saturated soil behaves differently from dry soil: it drains poorly, and it grips tree roots far less securely. This is why trees frequently fall during the second storm of a wet week rather than the first. The first storm did not knock them down, but it loosened the ground that held them.

The second is infiltration into buildings. Water that enters a wall through a small gap does not evaporate quickly. It moves through insulation and framing, and it can remain damp for weeks. Damage from this water often appears as a stain on a ceiling long after the sky has cleared, which makes it easy to blame on the wrong storm.

The third is overloaded storm drains. A drainage system is designed for a particular rate of flow. When rainfall exceeds that rate, water backs up and runs across streets and yards instead. As it runs, it carries oil, fertilizer, and sediment into creeks and rivers. The stream may look normal within a day, but the sediment settles into the gravel where fish lay eggs and remains there.

Cleanup crews are measured on how quickly the visible mess disappears. That measure is understandable, because the visible mess is what residents complain about. It is also incomplete. The branch across the sidewalk will be gone by Thursday. The saturated soil, the wet wall cavity, and the sediment in the creek gravel will still be doing their work in the spring.

## The Keeper of the Tide Pools (`g4-text-03`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 2

Every low tide, Oona counted the tide pools along the rock shelf, and every low tide she found the same thing: pool number seven was dying.

The others held anemones that opened like green flowers, small crabs that scuttled under ledges, and snails grazing slowly across the stone. Pool seven held silt, a few empty shells, and water that smelled wrong.

Oona decided to fix it. She scooped out the silt with a yogurt container. She carried in clean seawater bucket by bucket. She moved two anemones from pool four and set them carefully on the rock.

Within three days the anemones had shriveled. The silt was back. The water smelled worse than before.

Her aunt Ines, who had studied the shelf for twenty years, listened to the whole account without interrupting.

"Where does pool seven sit?" she asked.

"On the shelf. Same as the others."

"Show me."

They walked out at the next low tide. Ines did not look at the pool. She looked above it, at the cliff, where a thin seep of fresh water ran down the rock face and dripped steadily into pool seven and nowhere else.

"Fresh water," Ines said. "Every hour of every day. Those animals need salt."

Oona stared at the seep. She had walked past it a hundred times.

"So it cannot be fixed," she said.

"It does not need fixing," Ines said. "Look again."

Oona looked. In the shallow edge of pool seven, where nothing was supposed to live, a mat of pale green algae grew thick and soft, and among it moved small flat creatures she had never seen in any other pool.

"They are not in pool four," Ines said. "They cannot live in pool four. Pool seven is not a broken tide pool. You have been counting it wrong."

Oona knelt on the wet rock for a long time. Then she took out her notebook, crossed out the heading that said POOL 7 — DYING, and wrote a different one.

## The Boy Who Counted Crows (`g4-text-04`)

**Genre:** folktale-style original  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text in a traditional folktale style; not a reproduction of any existing tale. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 2

In a village at the edge of a wide field there lived a boy named Corin, who was known for one thing: he counted the crows.

Every morning he sat on the stone wall and counted them as they crossed from the woods to the field. Thirty-one. Twenty-eight. Thirty-three. The villagers laughed at him, kindly at first and then less kindly, because a boy of his age might have been mending nets or carrying water.

"What good is the number of crows?" the miller asked.

"I do not know yet," Corin said. "I only know it."

One autumn the count began to fall. Twenty-two. Nineteen. Fourteen. Corin told the miller, who told him crows were crows. He told the baker, who told him to carry water.

On the ninth day he counted four, and the four did not land in the field at all. They circled once and flew on toward the hills.

Corin walked to the field himself. He knelt and dug into the soil with his hands, and what he found there were grubs, thick and pale, more than he could hold, chewing at the roots of the winter wheat.

The crows had not abandoned the field. They had eaten what they could and gone looking for a field that still had something left in it.

Corin ran back to the village. This time the miller listened, because the number four is a hard thing to argue with when you have been told about thirty-one for two years.

The villagers turned the soil and saved most of the crop.

Afterward the baker asked Corin how he had known.

"I did not know," Corin said. "I counted. Knowing came later, and it could only come because I had been counting all along, back when it meant nothing at all."

## Marisol at the Radio Station (`g4-text-05`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction, first person  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 3

I had been at the station for three weeks before anyone let me touch the board.

My job was the log. Every song, every announcement, every station identification, written down with the time beside it. Sixty-two lines on a good afternoon. Ramona said the log was the most boring job in the building and also the one they could not run without, and I decided she was saying that to make me feel better.

On a Thursday in March the power flickered. The board went dark for maybe four seconds and came back up, and the automatic system that was supposed to remember what had been playing did not remember anything at all.

Ramona said a word I will not write down.

"We are off the log," she said. "We have no idea what we have played since two o'clock. If we repeat a sponsor spot we owe them money and if we skip one we owe them worse."

I held up my clipboard.

She looked at it the way you look at a door you did not know was in the wall.

We rebuilt the whole afternoon from my handwriting. Two twelve, sponsor spot, hardware store. Two nineteen, song. Two twenty-four, weather. It took eleven minutes and we did not miss a single one.

Afterward Ramona did not say thank you exactly. What she said was, "Sit here," and she moved over, and she showed me what the three left-hand faders did.

I want to be honest about something. For three weeks I had thought the log was busywork they gave to a kid so the kid would feel included. I wrote it carefully anyway, because that is how I was raised, not because I believed in it.

I believe in it now.

## The Same Thursday, Told from the Hallway (`g4-text-06`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction, third person  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 3

From the hallway outside Studio B, the power flicker on that Thursday in March lasted long enough for the emergency light above the fire door to click on and then off again.

The station manager, Desmond Ruiz, was walking toward the front office when it happened. He stopped, counted to five, and turned around.

He had been a broadcast engineer for nineteen years, and he already knew three things that the two people inside Studio B did not yet know. He knew the automation server did not write its playlist to disk until the top of the hour. He knew that meant roughly fifty minutes of programming had just ceased to exist as a record. And he knew the station's sponsor contracts required a verified log.

He reached the studio door and stopped with his hand on the handle, because through the glass he could see Ramona Fitch standing over the board with both hands pressed flat on it, and beside her the spring intern, holding up a clipboard.

Desmond watched for a moment. Then he took his hand off the handle and walked back to the front office.

His assistant asked him whether everything was all right in there.

"They have it," he said.

"You are sure?"

Desmond sat down. He thought about the nineteen years, and about how many times he had walked into a room to fix something that was already being fixed, and how long it had taken him to learn that walking in has a cost.

"The kid kept a paper log," he said. "Nobody told her to keep it that carefully. She just did."

He signed the incident report at four fifteen and wrote one sentence in the notes field: *No loss. Manual record maintained.*

## How a Water Treatment Plant Works (`g4-text-07`)

**Genre:** informational, process  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 4, 6

Water that reaches a household tap has passed through a treatment plant, where it is cleaned in a fixed sequence of stages. Each stage removes something the previous stage could not.

Water first arrives at the intake, drawn from a river, lake, or well. A coarse screen at the intake blocks leaves, sticks, fish, and debris. This step protects the equipment downstream, which is why it comes first.

Next comes coagulation. Operators add a chemical, often aluminum sulfate, that carries a positive electric charge. Most of the fine particles suspended in the water carry a negative charge, and because opposite charges attract, the particles stop repelling one another and begin to clump. Without this step the particles are far too small to remove.

The clumps then grow during flocculation, in which large paddles stir the water slowly. Slow stirring is essential. Vigorous stirring would tear the fragile clumps apart, so the goal is to bring particles into gentle contact until they form clusters heavy enough to sink.

In sedimentation, the water sits nearly still in a large basin for several hours. Gravity does the work: the heavy clusters settle to the bottom as sludge, which is removed separately. Most of the suspended material leaves the water here.

Filtration follows. The water passes downward through layers of sand, gravel, and often anthracite coal. These layers trap the particles too light to have settled out.

Finally comes disinfection. A disinfectant such as chlorine is added to kill bacteria and viruses that no physical filter can catch. A small amount is left in the water on purpose, so that the water remains protected while it travels through miles of pipe to homes.

The order cannot be rearranged. Filters would clog immediately without sedimentation, sedimentation would accomplish little without flocculation, and flocculation is impossible without coagulation first.

## Bringing Back the Kirtland's Warbler (`g4-text-08`)

**Genre:** informational, cause and effect  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 4, 5, 9

The Kirtland's warbler is a small songbird that nests almost entirely in the jack pine forests of northern Michigan. In the 1970s, fewer than three hundred singing males could be found. The reasons for the decline had little to do with the bird itself.

The first cause was fire suppression. Jack pine cones are sealed with resin and open mainly when heated by fire. Warblers nest on the ground beneath young jack pines between roughly five and twenty years old, where the low branches provide cover. For decades, every wildfire in the region was extinguished as quickly as possible. Fewer fires meant fewer new stands of jack pine, and the existing stands grew past the age the warbler can use.

The second cause was the brown-headed cowbird. Cowbirds do not build nests. A female lays her eggs in another bird's nest, and the host raises the cowbird chick, which hatches earlier and grows faster than the warbler chicks beside it. Cowbirds had expanded into Michigan as forests were cleared for farms, and by the 1970s a majority of warbler nests contained cowbird eggs.

The response addressed both causes at once. Foresters began planting jack pine in dense blocks and harvesting on a rotation, deliberately creating young stands where fire no longer did. Wildlife managers also began removing cowbirds from warbler nesting areas during the breeding season.

Neither action alone would have been sufficient. New habitat with heavy cowbird pressure would have produced nests that failed. Cowbird control without new habitat would have protected nests the birds had nowhere to build.

The population passed one thousand singing males in 2001 and has remained above two thousand in recent years. In 2019 the species was removed from the federal endangered species list, though management continues, because the habitat that supports it is still created by people rather than by fire.

## Three Ways to Move a City (`g4-text-09`)

**Genre:** informational, argument  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 7

When a growing city decides how to move people, it usually chooses among three approaches. Each one solves a real problem and creates a different one.

The first approach is to widen roads. This is the most popular choice, because the benefit appears immediately: more lanes carry more cars, and a commute that took thirty-five minutes may take twenty-five. The difficulty is a pattern transportation planners call induced demand. When driving becomes easier, more people choose to drive, and some who had moved closer to work move farther away. Studies of American metropolitan areas have repeatedly found that within about five to ten years, traffic volume rises roughly in proportion to the new lane capacity, and travel times return close to where they began.

The second approach is to build rail. A single light-rail line can carry far more people per hour than a lane of traffic, and it does so without adding vehicles to the street. The obstacle is cost and time. Rail requires purchasing land, relocating utilities, and years of construction before a single rider is served. A city facing congestion this year may be unwilling to spend heavily on a benefit that arrives in a decade, even when the arithmetic favors it.

The third approach is to improve buses. Buses use roads that already exist, so the cost is dramatically lower and service can begin within months. Giving buses a dedicated lane and signal priority produces much of the capacity benefit of rail at a fraction of the price. The obstacle here is political rather than technical: a dedicated bus lane takes a lane away from cars, and drivers can see exactly what they have lost while the benefit is spread across riders they never meet.

Notice that only one of these three obstacles is an engineering problem. The second is a budgeting problem, and the third is a problem of who is willing to give something up. A city that treats all three as engineering questions will keep widening roads and keep wondering why the traffic returns.

## The River Gauge: Reading the Numbers (`g4-text-10`)

**Genre:** informational with quantitative display  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 5, 9

A river gauge is a device that measures how high a river is at one location. The number it reports is called the gage height — spelled that way by the agencies that record it — and it is measured in feet above a fixed reference point, not above the riverbed.

The gauge on the Kestrel River at Millbrook reports a reading every fifteen minutes. The following description presents one week of daily peak readings in words, so that the information is available whether or not a chart is displayed.

On Monday the peak gage height was 3.1 feet. On Tuesday it was 3.0 feet. On Wednesday it rose sharply to 7.4 feet. On Thursday it reached its highest point of the week at 8.7 feet. On Friday it fell to 6.8 feet. On Saturday it fell further to 4.5 feet. On Sunday it measured 3.6 feet.

Two features of this pattern are worth noticing.

First, the rise is much steeper than the fall. The river climbed more than five feet in two days and was still falling three days later. This shape is typical. Rain enters a river quickly through surface runoff, but the water stored in saturated soil drains out slowly for days afterward.

Second, the river did not return to its starting level. Sunday's 3.6 feet remains half a foot above Monday's 3.1 feet, because the ground is still releasing water it absorbed during the storm.

The National Weather Service assigns each gauge a flood stage, which for Millbrook is 8.0 feet. Thursday's peak of 8.7 feet therefore exceeded flood stage by 0.7 feet.

A reader should be careful here. Flood stage is not the height at which water enters the river. It is the height at which water begins to affect roads, property, or people at that particular location. A gage height of 8.7 feet would mean something entirely different on a different river.

## Why a Folded Sheet Beats a Flat One (`g4-text-11`)

**Genre:** explanatory model  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 6

Lay a flat sheet of paper across a gap between two books and it collapses under the weight of a single coin. Fold the same sheet into a row of narrow accordion pleats, lay it across the same gap, and it will hold a stack of coins. Nothing has been added. Only the shape has changed.

The explanation involves how a material resists bending. When a flat sheet bends downward, its top surface is squeezed and its bottom surface is stretched. The material closest to the middle of the sheet does almost nothing, because it is neither squeezed nor stretched. A flat sheet is thin, so nearly all of its material sits near that inactive middle.

Folding changes where the material sits. In a pleated sheet, some of the paper is lifted well above the center line and some is pushed well below it. Material located far from the center resists bending far more effectively than material near it. Engineers describe this using a quantity called the second moment of area, which increases sharply as material moves away from the middle.

This is why structural beams are shaped like the capital letter I. Most of the steel is concentrated in the top and bottom flanges, far from the center, while the thin vertical web in the middle mainly holds those flanges apart. An I-beam uses less steel than a solid rectangular bar of equal strength.

The same principle explains corrugated cardboard, ridged metal roofing, and the fluted edge of a pie crust. In each case a weak, thin material is made stiff not by adding more of it, but by moving what is already there farther from the center.

## A Case for Later Library Hours (`g4-text-12`)

**Genre:** opinion model  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. Millbrook, its library, and the service report cited are fictional; the statistics are illustrative and are not drawn from any real report. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 7

The Millbrook branch library should extend its weekday hours from five o'clock to seven o'clock.

My strongest reason is that the current schedule excludes the households the library was built to serve. According to the branch's own 2025 service report, 61 percent of Millbrook residents work shifts ending at five o'clock or later. A library that locks its doors at five is open to the community only during the hours most of the community is unavailable.

My second reason is that the demand is already visible in the branch's numbers. The same report shows that the final hour of operation, four to five o'clock, accounts for 24 percent of weekday visits, more than any other single hour. When a service is busiest at the moment it shuts down, that is evidence of demand that is being cut off rather than demand that has been met.

My third reason is that the fixed costs are already paid. The building, the collection, the computers, and the internet connection cost the same whether the doors are open at six o'clock or locked. Extending hours purchases additional public use of an asset the public has already bought.

Some residents argue that the money would be better spent on new books rather than on staffing two extra hours. This is a fair objection, and it is not based on a misunderstanding. A collection that never grows does eventually stop drawing readers.

I would answer it this way. A book that cannot be borrowed because the building is closed provides no service at all, and 61 percent of residents currently cannot borrow it on a weekday. Expanding access to the entire existing collection reaches more readers than adding a small number of new titles to a collection most residents cannot get to.

The books are already on the shelves. The question is who is allowed through the door.

My grandmother draws maps of a country
that is not on any map.

## The Dispatcher: A Short Scene (`g4-text-14`)

**Genre:** drama  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy dramatic scene. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.

CAST
HARLAN VOSS, a railroad dispatcher, late fifties
PRIYA NAIR, a dispatcher in training

SETTING: A dispatch office. A wall-length track diagram glows behind them. A phone handset rests on the desk. Late night.

HARLAN: Read me the board.

PRIYA: Westbound 44 is at milepost 118. Eastbound 17 is at 131. Single track between them.

HARLAN: And?

PRIYA: And the siding at Corliss is at 126.

HARLAN: So?

PRIYA: So one of them takes the siding at Corliss and the other one goes through.

HARLAN: Which one.

PRIYA (pause): The eastbound. Seventeen.

HARLAN: Why.

PRIYA: Because... it is closer to Corliss?

HARLAN (not unkindly): That is arithmetic. Give me a reason.

PRIYA: (studying the board) Forty-four is a hundred and ten cars of grain. Seventeen is eighteen cars. If I stop 44 on a grade it may not start again.

HARLAN: Now you are dispatching. (He picks up the handset, then stops, holding it.) One more thing. What is the Corliss siding length?

PRIYA: Forty-two hundred feet.

HARLAN: And 44?

PRIYA (going pale): Longer. Forty-four does not fit in Corliss.

HARLAN: It does not. It never has. Nothing on the board tells you that. (He sets the handset down.) The board shows you where things are. It does not show you what they are.

PRIYA: Then how does anybody learn it?

HARLAN: Somebody tells you. (beat) Somebody told me, in this chair, in 1994, and he was standing about where you are standing.

(He slides the handset across the desk to her.)

HARLAN: Make the call. It is yours.

(PRIYA looks at the board a long moment. She picks up the handset. Lights fade.)

## The Weight of the Bucket (`g4-text-15`)

**Genre:** narrative model  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 10

The maple sap bucket held four gallons when it was full, which was thirty-two pounds, which was more than Dev could carry level.

He had asked for the job. That was the part he kept returning to as he shuffled down the slope with the bucket banging against his shin and sap slopping over the rim in bright cold splashes.

His older sister Anita carried two buckets and did not slop.

"You are fighting it," she said, without turning around.

"I am carrying it."

"You are fighting it. Watch." She stopped and set both buckets down in the snow. "Hold it away from your leg. Let your arm hang straight. Do not try to keep it still."

"If I do not keep it still it will spill."

"It spills because you keep stopping it."

That made no sense at all, so Dev tried it out of irritation. He let his arm go loose. The bucket swung forward, back, forward, in time with his steps, and the sap inside rocked and rode the swing instead of climbing the sides.

He walked forty feet without spilling a drop.

"Huh," he said.

Anita picked up her buckets. "Dad taught me that. Grandpa taught him. Nobody figured it out. Somebody just says it to you and then you know it."

They emptied into the collection tank at the bottom of the hill, and Dev's arms were shaking, and the sap ran out of the bucket in a long amber rope that steamed slightly in the cold.

On the walk back up he thought about the fact that the trick was three sentences long. Three sentences, and a hundred years of people saying them to each other on this hill, and without them he would have carried buckets badly for the rest of his life and called it being tired.

He was still tired. But he was not fighting it anymore.

## What the Year Taught Me (`g4-text-16`)

**Genre:** reflective narrative  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 10

In September I thought revising meant fixing spelling.

I would write a paragraph, read it once for red underlines, and call it finished. When my teacher wrote *develop this* in the margin, I genuinely did not know what she was asking for. The sentence was correct. What else was there?

The answer arrived in November, and it arrived through a piece I did not care about.

We had to explain a process. I chose how a lock works on a canal, because my uncle had shown me one. I wrote four sentences. My partner read them and asked why the water does not simply run out the bottom. I said because of the gates. She asked how the gates hold back that much water. I said because they are strong. She said that was not an answer, and she was right, and I was annoyed for about a day.

Then I went and found out. The gates form a shallow V pointing upstream, so the water pressure pushes them together instead of apart. The harder the water pushes, the tighter they seal.

When I put that in, the paragraph stopped being four correct sentences and started being something a person could learn from.

That is what *develop this* meant. It took me two months and one annoying question to find out.

In March I wrote an opinion piece and someone asked me a question I could not answer, and I recognized the feeling immediately. I did not get annoyed. I went and found out.

I still misspell *necessary*. I expect I always will. But I no longer think that is what revising is.

## Firsthand: The Night the Levee Held (`g4-text-17`)

**Genre:** informational, firsthand account  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text written as a firsthand account. The narrator and the events are fictional; the text is not a reproduction of any real diary or record. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 7

From the log kept by the levee keeper at Millbrook, written the following morning.

I went out at eleven with a lantern because I could hear the river and I have never been able to hear the river from the porch before.

The gage read 8.6 feet at eleven fifteen. Flood stage is 8.0. I walked the south wall first because that is the low section and the section I worry about.

At the third culvert I found water coming through on the dry side. Not much. About what a garden hose puts out. I want to write down that I did not panic, but I want to write down honestly that my hands were not steady when I set the lantern on the wall.

What you look for is whether the water coming through is clear or dirty. Clear water is seepage and seepage is ordinary. Dirty water means the wall itself is washing away inside and you have very little time.

I knelt down and put the lantern near it. It ran clear.

I sat with it until two in the morning and it stayed clear and it did not get bigger.

The gage peaked at 9.2 around four. I was there. I did not sleep.

In the morning three men from the county came out and looked at the culvert for about ten minutes and told me it was ordinary seepage.

I know it was. I knew it at midnight. That is not the same as knowing it at midnight by yourself with a lantern.

## Secondhand: The Millbrook Flood of 1954 (`g4-text-18`)

**Genre:** informational, secondhand account  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text written as a secondhand historical account. The town, river, and events are fictional; the text is not a reproduction of any real history. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 7

Adapted from a local history of Millbrook.

The flood of April 1954 remains the highest water recorded on the Kestrel River at Millbrook. Steady rain over four days saturated the watershed, and the gage height at the Millbrook station peaked at 9.2 feet on the morning of April 14, exceeding the 8.0-foot flood stage by more than a foot.

The river did not overtop the south levee. Damage in the town was limited to eleven flooded basements and the loss of a footbridge downstream of the mill.

Several factors account for the outcome. The levee had been reinforced in 1949 following an engineering survey, and the county had cleared debris from the culverts the previous autumn. The peak also arrived relatively quickly and receded within four days, which limited the time the wall was under load.

Contemporary accounts note that the levee keeper on duty, a county employee, remained on the wall through the night and reported seepage at the third culvert. County engineers inspected the site on the morning of April 14 and classified the seepage as normal for a structure of that design under those conditions.

The 1954 flood is often cited in later county planning documents as evidence that the 1949 reinforcement was sound. It is worth noting that the same documents record no comparable test of the north wall, which was not reinforced until 1971.

Millbrook has exceeded flood stage nine times since 1954. The 1954 peak has not been matched.
