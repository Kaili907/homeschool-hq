## The Bus-Stop Library (`g3-text-01`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 1

The shelter at the corner of Nash Street had a bench, a roof, and nothing to read. Ivy noticed this on a Tuesday, while she waited eleven minutes for the number 12.

She mentioned it at dinner. Her uncle said that a person could not simply put a bookshelf at a bus stop. Rain would ruin the books. Wind would knock them over. Somebody would have to look after them.

Ivy thought about all three problems for a week. Then she asked the hardware store for a wooden crate, turned it on its side, and screwed a hinged lid across the open front. She lined the inside with a plastic bag cut flat. On the lid she painted six words: TAKE ONE. LEAVE ONE. STAY DRY.

The first day, she put in four books she had already finished twice. The second day, three of them were gone and a thin paperback about beetles had appeared. By the end of the month, the crate held books Ivy had never chosen and would never have chosen. She read the beetle book anyway. It turned out that beetles were far stranger than she expected.

Her uncle still said a person could not put a bookshelf at a bus stop. He said it, though, while returning a book to the crate.

Ivy did not argue. She had learned something better than being right: a problem with three parts can be solved three parts at a time.

## How a Seed Finds the Light (`g3-text-02`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 1, 4

A seed begins its life underground, in the dark. It has no leaves and cannot yet make food. Everything it needs for its first days is packed inside the seed itself.

When the soil is warm enough and wet enough, the seed takes in water and swells. The seed coat splits. A root pushes downward first. This matters, because the root anchors the young plant and begins pulling in water. Only after the root is established does a shoot push upward.

The shoot travels through dark soil without any way to see. Instead, it senses gravity. Special cells inside the stem detect which way is down, and the shoot grows the opposite way. Scientists call this response gravitropism.

Once the shoot breaks the surface, a second signal takes over. The stem begins to grow toward light. If a plant sits on a windowsill, it will lean toward the glass within a day or two. Turning the pot causes the plant to straighten and then lean again.

The first leaves finally open, and the plant begins making its own food from light, air, and water. From that moment on, the packed food inside the seed is no longer needed.

A seed, then, does not find the light by luck. It uses one signal to get out of the ground and a different signal to reach the sun.

## The Word Collector (`g3-text-03`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 2

Teo kept a jar of words on his desk. Not the jar you are imagining, full of paper scraps. His was a list taped to the side of an old jam jar, and inside the jar he kept pebbles, one for each word he could finally read without stopping.

The trouble was always the long ones. Unbelievable. Disagreement. Carefully. When Teo hit a long word, his eyes slid off it the way a shoe slides on ice.

His reading teacher showed him a different move. "Do not swallow the whole word," she said. "Find a part you already own."

In unbelievable, Teo found believe sitting in the middle, un at the front, and able at the end. Un meant not. Able meant can be. Not able to be believed. He read it, and it stayed read.

After that, Teo went hunting. He found agree inside disagreement and care inside carefully. He found play inside playful, inside replay, and inside playground, and the same four letters meant the same thing every time.

By spring the jar was heavy. Teo poured the pebbles out on the table and counted eighty-four.

"Eighty-four words," his teacher said.

"No," Teo said. "Eighty-four keys. The words they open are a lot more than that."

## The Parts That Change a Word (`g3-text-04`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 2

A long word is often a short word wearing extra pieces. Once a reader learns the pieces, the long word stops being frightening.

Some pieces go on the front. Un means not, so unkind means not kind and unlocked means not locked. Re means again, so retell means tell again and rebuild means build again. Pre means before, so preheat means heat before. Dis often works like un, so disagree means not agree.

Other pieces go on the end. Ful means full of, so hopeful means full of hope. Less means without, so hopeless means without hope. Notice that hopeful and hopeless share the same small word and point in opposite directions. Ly usually tells how something is done, so slowly tells how a person walked. Ness turns a describing word into a thing, so kindness is the quality of being kind.

A few endings come to English from Latin, and they show up constantly in school books. Ment makes a thing out of an action, as in payment, movement, and agreement. Tion and sion do something similar, as in action, invention, and decision. Able means can be, as in washable and readable.

The useful move is always the same. When a word looks too long, cover the front piece and the end piece with a finger and read what is left. If the middle is a word you know, put the pieces back one at a time and check whether the meaning still fits the sentence.

This will not work on every word. English borrowed from too many places to be perfectly tidy. But it works often enough that a reader who tries it will get through most long words without help, and getting through without help is the whole point.

## Nadia and the Stubborn Gate (`g3-text-05`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 3

The gate behind the community garden had not opened properly in two years. It scraped, caught, and then stopped, leaving a gap too narrow for a wheelbarrow. Most people lifted their tools over the fence instead.

Nadia did not like lifting things over fences. She liked knowing why something was broken.

She crouched and looked at the bottom of the gate, then at the ground beneath it. The dirt there was worn into a shallow curve, as though the gate had been sanding the earth for years. She pushed the gate slowly and watched. The lowest corner dragged.

Her neighbor Mr. Adeyemi told her the gate needed replacing and that this was that. He had said the same thing about the hose, the bench, and the shed door.

Nadia measured the drag with her hand: about two fingers of sag. Then she found the top hinge and saw three screws where there should have been four. The empty hole was stripped and round.

It took her four afternoons. She filled the stripped hole with wooden matchsticks and glue, waited a day, drilled a new pilot hole, and drove in a longer screw. The gate lifted almost a full inch.

It still scraped a little. Nadia decided that a gate that scraped a little and opened all the way was a good enough gate.

When Mr. Adeyemi wheeled the first barrow through, he did not say anything about replacing it. He only asked whether she had any of those matchsticks left, because his shed door was worse.

## Nadia and the Missing Key (`g3-text-06`)

**Genre:** realistic fiction  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 3

The garden shed key went missing in July, and eleven people had reasons to blame each other.

Nadia listened to all eleven. Then she did what she always did, which was to stop listening to people and start looking at things.

The key had hung on a nail inside the shed door. The nail was still there. Below it, on the floor, was a stripe of dust with a clean gap in the middle, the shape of something that had fallen and then been moved.

She asked one question: who had swept?

Mrs. Okonjo had swept, on Sunday, into a dustpan, into the yard-waste bag, which was now somewhere in a truck in another part of the county. The key was gone for good.

Nadia did not announce that she had solved it. Announcing it would have made Mrs. Okonjo feel terrible over an ordinary broom stroke. Instead she said the key had probably fallen and been swept out by accident, and that the fix was to stop hanging it over a floor.

She drilled a small hole in the handle of a scrap of wood, tied the spare key to it, and hung the whole thing high, where nothing could knock it down.

"That is ugly," said Mr. Adeyemi.

"It is," Nadia agreed. "But you can see it from the gate, and nobody will ever sweep it away."

The ugly wooden tag hung there for years. Nobody lost the key again.

## Why Bees Dance (`g3-text-07`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 4, 6

A honeybee that finds a good patch of flowers faces a problem. The patch may be far from the hive, and the bee cannot lead thousands of other bees there one at a time. Instead, it dances.

Back inside the dark hive, the returning bee moves across the honeycomb in a repeating pattern. Other bees crowd close and follow the movement with their antennae. The dance carries real information.

The direction of the dance tells the other bees which way to fly. Because the hive is dark and vertical, the bee cannot simply point at the sun. Instead, straight up on the honeycomb stands for the direction of the sun. If the flowers are forty degrees to the right of the sun, the dancing bee angles forty degrees to the right of straight up.

The length of the dance tells the other bees how far to fly. A longer run means a longer trip. A very short dance, done in tight circles, usually means the food is close to home.

The energy of the dance carries a third message. A bee that has found an excellent patch dances harder and repeats the pattern more times. A weaker patch produces a shorter, calmer dance, and fewer bees are convinced to go.

The result is a hive that sends most of its workers to the best food available, without any single bee being in charge of the decision.

## The Night Sky Notebook (`g3-text-08`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 4, 9

Anyone can study the night sky with nothing but a notebook, a pencil, and patience. Astronomers did exactly this for thousands of years before telescopes existed.

Start by choosing one spot and returning to it. Standing in the same place each night means that a tree, a roofline, or a pole can serve as a fixed marker. Without a marker, it is nearly impossible to tell whether an object has moved.

Next, record the date and the time. The sky turns slowly all night and also shifts from month to month. A drawing without a time written beside it cannot be compared with any other drawing.

Then sketch what you see above the marker. Sketches do not need to be beautiful. They need to show which points of light are brighter, and roughly how far apart they sit.

After a week, patterns begin to appear. Most points of light hold the same positions relative to one another. These are stars. A few points wander slowly against that background from night to night. These are planets, and the ancient Greek word for wanderer is where the word planet comes from.

The moon changes fastest of all, shifting position and shape night by night in a cycle of about a month.

None of this requires equipment. It requires only the same spot, the same notebook, and enough nights to let a pattern show itself.

## How a Footbridge Gets Built (`g3-text-09`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 5, 7

Building a footbridge over a creek looks simple from the outside. The work happens in a fixed order, and skipping a step causes problems later.

First, workers study the ground on both banks. Soft soil cannot hold weight. If the soil is soft, crews dig down until they reach firmer material, because everything above depends on what is below.

Second, they build the foundations, called abutments, one on each bank. Concrete is poured into forms and left to cure. Curing takes days, and hurrying it produces weak concrete. This is why a bridge site often looks abandoned for a week.

Third, the beams are set. Beams span from one abutment to the other and carry the load across the gap. They are usually placed by crane in a single morning, which is the part people stop to watch.

Fourth, the decking goes down across the beams. Deck boards give walkers a surface and are fastened so they cannot shift.

Fifth, railings are attached. Railings are added last because workers need open edges while they fasten the deck.

Finally, the bridge is loaded and tested before anyone is allowed to cross.

The order matters because each step depends on the one before it. Railings cannot be attached to decking that is not yet down, decking cannot rest on beams that are not yet set, and beams cannot sit on concrete that has not yet cured.

## Two Looks at the Same River (`g3-text-10`)

**Genre:** informational  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 5, 9

The Kestrel River runs for sixty miles, and two different books describe it in two different ways.

A guide for paddlers describes the river as a route. It lists the places to put a boat in the water and take it out. It warns about a stretch of fast water below the old mill, notes where the current slows, and explains that the river drops about four feet per mile in its upper half. Its purpose is to help a person travel the river safely, so it organizes everything from upstream to downstream.

A book about local wildlife describes the same river as a habitat. It explains that cold, fast water near the headwaters holds trout, while the slower, warmer water downstream holds bass and turtles. It describes the herons that hunt the shallow edges and the mussels buried in the gravel. Its purpose is to explain who lives where and why, so it organizes everything by habitat rather than by distance.

Both books are accurate. Neither is complete. The paddling guide barely mentions the mussels, and the wildlife book says almost nothing about where to launch a canoe.

A reader who wants to understand the Kestrel River needs both. The fast water below the old mill is a hazard in one book and a trout habitat in the other, and it is genuinely both at once. What a text tells you depends a great deal on what it was written to do.

## Compost in a Bucket (`g3-text-11`)

**Genre:** explanatory model  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 6

Composting turns kitchen scraps into soil, and it can be done in a single bucket on a porch.

Compost needs four things. It needs greens, which are wet scraps such as fruit peels and vegetable ends. It needs browns, which are dry materials such as shredded paper, dry leaves, or sawdust. It also needs air and a small amount of water. Leaving out any one of the four slows everything down.

Start by drilling several small holes near the top of a five-gallon bucket so air can move. Put down a layer of browns about as thick as your hand. Add greens on top, then cover the greens with more browns. Covering matters, because uncovered food scraps attract insects and smell unpleasant.

Stir the bucket about twice a week. Stirring moves air into the middle, where the material breaks down fastest. If the mixture smells sour, there are too many greens, and adding browns will fix it. If nothing seems to be happening at all, the mixture is probably too dry, and a small amount of water will help.

After several weeks, the material at the bottom turns dark, crumbly, and earthy-smelling. That is finished compost, and it can be mixed into a garden bed or a flowerpot.

Nothing about the process is complicated. It is mostly a matter of balance and patience.

## Should the Library Stay Open Later? (`g3-text-12`)

**Genre:** opinion model  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 7

The Nash Street branch library closes at five o'clock. I think it should stay open until seven on weekdays.

My first reason is that many families cannot reach the library before five. Adults who work until five simply cannot bring a child to a building that is already locked. A library that is only open while people are at work and at school is not truly open to them.

My second reason is that the library is the only quiet, free indoor space in this neighborhood. There is no charge to sit at a table, and nobody is asked to buy anything. When it closes, students who do not have a quiet place at home lose the only one available to them.

My third reason is that the building is already there. The shelves, the tables, the internet, and the books do not disappear at five o'clock. Keeping the doors open for two more hours uses something the neighborhood has already paid for.

Two hours on weekdays would reach the families who are currently shut out. A library serves the people who can walk through its doors, and right now the doors close before many of them can get there.

## Rain on the Tin Roof (`g3-text-13`)

**Genre:** poem  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy poem. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.

First one drop, a single tap,
like someone knocking to come in.

Then four, then twelve, then far too many
to count them out or catch a penny
of the sound. The roof turns drum.
The gutters gargle. Here they come.

A thousand tiny running feet
that never once have learned to meet
the ground politely, one by one.
They fall the way that things get done
when everybody starts at once.

And then, as quickly as they came,
the feet run off. The roof goes tame.
One drop. One tap. The roof is dry.
The gutter clears its throat. Goodbye.

## The Lost Mitten: A Short Scene (`g3-text-14`)

**Genre:** drama  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy dramatic scene. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 8

CAST
PAZ, a third grader
WREN, her cousin
MR. HALE, a crossing guard

SETTING: A street corner on a cold morning. A traffic signal blinks. PAZ is searching her coat pockets.

PAZ: It was here. It was in this pocket. Pockets do not have opinions.

WREN: Pockets have holes.

PAZ (checking): This one does not have a hole. (pause) This one has a hole.

WREN: Then it is somewhere between your house and this corner.

PAZ: That is four blocks of somewhere.

MR. HALE (raising his stop sign): Morning. Do not step off the curb yet.

WREN: She lost a mitten.

MR. HALE: Red one? Stripe on the thumb?

PAZ (turning quickly): Yes.

MR. HALE: It is on the fence post outside the bakery. Somebody put it up where it could be seen. (He waves them across.) That is what people do here. If a thing falls, it goes on a post.

PAZ (crossing, half to herself): On a post.

WREN: So the street was helping you the whole time and you did not know.

PAZ: The street did not sew my pocket.

WREN: No. That part is still yours.

(They exit toward the bakery. MR. HALE lowers the sign. Lights fade.)

## The Longest Walk (`g3-text-15`)

**Genre:** narrative model  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 10

The walk from the school door to the front of the room was eleven steps. Malik had counted them twice while pretending to look for a pencil.

He was supposed to read his paragraph aloud. He had written it about his grandfather's toolbox, and he liked it, which was the problem. Liking it meant it could be ruined.

"Whenever you are ready," said Ms. Boone.

Malik stood. The eleven steps took a long time. At the front, the paper shook slightly, so he set it on the podium and held the edges instead.

He read the first sentence too fast. He heard himself do it, stopped, and did something he had not planned. He started the sentence over.

The second time, it came out at the right speed.

He read about the toolbox with the dent in the lid, and the screwdriver worn smooth in one spot where his grandfather's thumb had rested for thirty years. He read the last line, which he had rewritten four times: *A tool remembers the hand that used it.*

Then it was quiet, which he had not expected, and then people clapped, which he had also not expected.

On the way back to his seat, Malik did not count. He was thinking about the moment he had stopped and started over, in front of everyone, and how nobody had laughed.

That was the part he wrote about that night. Not the clapping. The starting over.

## A Reader's Year (`g3-text-16`)

**Genre:** reflective narrative  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy text. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 10

In September, Priya read the way a person crosses a stream on wobbling stones: one word, then a pause, then the next word, with all her attention on not falling.

By November, something had changed in a way she could not name. She noticed it while reading a chapter about a girl and a lost dog. She reached the bottom of a page and realized she had not thought about the words at all. She had been thinking about the dog.

In January she got stuck. The book she chose used long words about weather, and she slid off them the way she used to slide off everything. She almost quit. Instead she did what she had practiced: she covered the ending, found the part she knew, and read it in pieces. Atmosphere. Temperature. Precipitation. The words held still.

In April she reread the first book she had finished in the fall. It took her one afternoon instead of three weeks, and she noticed a joke on page nine that she had missed entirely the first time.

In June her teacher asked what she had learned this year.

Priya thought about wobbling stones, and about the dog, and about the word precipitation.

"I learned that getting stuck is not the same as being finished," she said.

## The Two Buckets (`g3-text-17`)

**Genre:** fable-style original  
**Source type:** original  
**Rights:** Original Manuel Academy fable. All rights reserved by Manuel Academy.  
**Used in units:** 3

Two buckets hung on the same rope above the same well, one going down whenever the other came up.

The first bucket complained without stopping. "What a miserable arrangement," it said. "No matter how full I come up, I am always sent back down empty. All that work, and I end exactly where I started."

The second bucket had heard this many times. "I have been thinking about the same rope," it said, "and I notice something different. No matter how empty I go down, I am always brought back up full."

"Those are the same thing," said the first bucket.

"They are," the second bucket agreed. "We do exactly the same work. But you spend the whole day dreading the trip down, and I spend the whole day expecting the trip up. At the end of it we have both carried the same water, and only one of us was miserable."

The first bucket said nothing for a while. Then it said, "That does not change the rope."

"No," said the second bucket. "It changes the day."

The well-keeper, who knew nothing of the conversation, worked until sundown and hung both buckets on their hooks. They had drawn the same amount of water. They had made the same number of trips. And each of them had lived a completely different day.

The lesson of this story is that two people can do identical work and have opposite experiences of it, because what a person expects at the start of a trip shapes what the trip feels like.
