## Week 1 — Your Brain Trains Like a Muscle

**Core:** Nobody is born knowing how to hit a switch or escape side control. The people who are good at those things built that skill, rep by rep — and their brains literally changed while they did it. Scientists can watch it happen: when you practice something hard, your brain grows new connections, the way your muscles grow from lifting. That means "I'm bad at this" is never the end of a sentence. The true version is "I'm bad at this *yet*." The word *yet* is the most honest word in sports. You will be bad at every skill you ever learn — first. That's not a problem with you. That's just what the start looks like.

**Littles:** Your brain is like a muscle. Every time you practice something hard — a cartwheel, a takedown, your times tables — your brain gets a tiny bit stronger at it, just like your arms get stronger from climbing. So if something is hard today, that doesn't mean you're bad at it. It means you're not good at it YET. "Yet" is a magic word. Try adding it: "I can't do this... yet."

**Teens extra:** This has a name — growth mindset — and the research behind it is some of the most replicated in sports psychology. The practical edge it gives you: athletes who believe skill is built treat hard practices as construction; athletes who believe talent is fixed treat hard practices as exposure — evidence they might not "have it." Same practice, totally different experience. Watch which one your brain defaults to this week. You can't choose your first thought, but you can choose your second.

**Habit:** Catch yourself thinking or saying "I can't do this" — and add "yet."
**Reflect (littles, one word):** What's one thing you can't do YET?
**Reflect:** What's something you're currently bad at — in sport or school — and what would change about how it feels if you truly believed "yet"?

## Week 2 — Hard Is the Point

**Core:** Here's a secret about drilling: the boring, repetitive, slightly-too-hard part IS the training. It's not the price you pay before the fun part — it's the machine that builds you. Easy reps feel nice and change almost nothing. Hard reps feel clumsy and change everything. Scientists who study experts call this deliberate practice: working right at the edge where you mess up a lot. If practice feels smooth all the time, you're rehearsing what you already own. Growth lives just past comfortable.

**Littles:** When practice feels hard, your brain is growing the most. Easy stuff is fun, but it doesn't make you stronger — it's like lifting a feather. The wobbly, tricky, "ugh, again?!" stuff is the feather turning into a real weight. Hard means it's working.

**Teens extra:** The trap at your level is practicing your A-game forever because it feels good and looks good in the room. Champions do the opposite: they spend their practice time in their weakest positions on purpose. This week, notice the drill or position you quietly avoid. That avoidance is a map with an X on it.

**Habit:** Pick the drill or position you like least — and go at it on purpose this week.
**Reflect (littles, emoji):** How did the hard drill feel by the end of the week?
**Reflect:** What do you avoid in training, and what specifically makes you avoid it — boredom, frustration, or fear of looking bad?

## Week 3 — Courage Is a Rep

**Core:** Courage isn't a personality type. It's a rep — a thing you do, small, over and over, until doing scary things becomes your normal. Going first when nobody wants to. Asking the question everyone's afraid to ask. Rolling with the partner who's better than you. Each small brave act makes the next one easier, exactly like each rep makes the next one lighter. Nobody feels brave before doing the brave thing. The feeling comes after. You act first; courage catches up.

**Littles:** Being brave doesn't mean you're not scared. It means you do the thing WHILE a little scared. And here's the trick: every brave thing you do makes the next brave thing easier. Brave is a muscle too.

**Teens extra:** Discomfort tolerance might be the single most transferable skill your sport gives you. The girl who can stay calm in a bad position on the mat is training the same system she'll use in a job interview, a hard conversation, a college exam. You're not just learning to wrestle. You're learning to be okay where it's uncomfortable — which is where every interesting thing in life happens.

**Habit:** Volunteer first for one thing at practice this week.
**Reflect (littles, one word):** What brave thing did you do this week?
**Reflect:** Describe one courage rep you took this week and what happened right after — in your body, and in the room.

## Week 4 — The Two Lists

**Core:** Everything at a competition belongs to one of two lists. List one — yours: your effort, your attitude, your focus, your response to what happens, your preparation. List two — not yours: your opponent's skill, the ref's calls, the bracket, the noise, and yes, the final score. Frustration usually comes from grabbing at list two — trying to control things that were never controllable. Calm and confidence come from pouring everything into list one. The strange math of sports: the athletes who obsess least about the outcome, and most about their own list, win more.

**Littles:** Some things are yours to control: how hard you try, how you act, how you treat people. Some things aren't: who you face, what the ref says, who wins. When you feel mad or worried, ask: "Is this on MY list?" If it's not, let it go and get back to your list.

**Teens extra:** This is stoicism, two thousand years old, and it's the backbone of every serious mental performance system. Try running the sort in real time this week: when frustration hits, name the thing — "ref's call, list two" — and physically redirect to a list-one action (breath, stance, next exchange). The naming is what breaks the spiral.

**Habit:** When something frustrates you this week, silently ask: which list?
**Reflect (littles, emoji):** How did it feel to let go of a "not-my-list" thing?
**Reflect:** What list-two thing steals the most energy from you — and what list-one thing deserves that energy instead?

## Week 5 — Mistakes Are Information

**Core:** Champions watch film of themselves losing. On purpose. Repeatedly. Not to feel bad — to mine it. Every mistake carries a message: your weight was wrong, your grip broke first, you hesitated. The mistake already happened; the only question left is whether you collect the information inside it. Athletes who treat mistakes as verdicts hide from them and repeat them. Athletes who treat mistakes as data study them and delete them. Same mistake, two futures.

**Littles:** A mistake is like a treasure map — it shows you exactly where to dig. Missed the move? Now you know which part to practice! Kids who look at their mistakes get better way faster than kids who hide from them.

**Teens extra:** You already live inside this system — your gradebook literally sorts every error into "skill gap" vs. "careless." That's an information system, not a judgment system. Apply the same sort on the mat: was that a technique I don't own yet, or a technique I own and rushed? The fix for each is completely different, and knowing which is which is half of coaching yourself.

**Habit:** After each practice, find one mistake and name the one thing it taught you.
**Reflect (littles, one word):** What did a mistake teach you this week?
**Reflect:** Write down one recurring mistake of yours and sort it: skill gap or careless? What's the specific fix for that type?

## Week 6 — You Are Not Your Record

**Core:** Here is something true that big-time athletes take years to learn: your results are something you *produce*, not something you *are*. A scoreboard measures one contest on one day between two people who both trained hard. It does not measure your worth, your future, or how much you matter to the people who love you — those were never on the table. When you know this deep down, something unexpected happens: you compete *better*. Athletes who feel their whole self is at stake tighten up and protect. Athletes who know they're safe either way stay loose and attack. Being more than your record isn't just true — it's a competitive advantage.

**Littles:** If you win, you're you. If you lose, you're still you — same girl, same family, same worth. Winning and losing are things that HAPPEN to you sometimes. They are not what you ARE. And funny enough, kids who know this play braver.

**Teens extra:** Watch for identity fusion this season — the feeling that a loss is a statement about who you are, or that your standing in the family or the room rides on results. It's the root of competition-as-threat, and this year (college applications, transcripts, brackets) will test it. Build the counterweight deliberately: keep a list of five things you are that no scoreboard can touch, and read it before you compete.

**Habit:** Write (or think of) five things you are that have nothing to do with any scoreboard.
**Reflect (littles, one word):** Name one thing you are that isn't about winning.
**Reflect:** What are your five? Which one do you forget fastest on a bad day?

## Week 7 — The Reset Breath

**Core:** Your breath is the one dial on your nervous system you can turn by hand. When you're rattled — bad exchange, bad call, nerves spiking — your breathing goes short and high, and your brain reads that as *emergency*, which makes everything worse. Box breathing reverses it: in for 4 counts, hold 4, out for 4, hold 4. Two or three boxes and your heart rate drops, your vision widens, your thinking comes back. It works in a timeout, between periods, before a test, anywhere. It's not relaxation for its own sake — it's taking the controls back.

**Littles:** Try dragon breathing: breathe in slowly through your nose for 4 counts like you're filling up with dragon fire... hold it 4... breathe out slow for 4... hold 4. Do it three times. Feel how your body calms down? That's YOUR superpower — you can use it anytime you feel wobbly.

**Teens extra:** The physiology: extended exhales activate the parasympathetic system — the braking system — directly. That's why it works when "just calm down" doesn't: it's mechanical, not motivational. Advanced version for acute spikes: two quick inhales through the nose, one long sigh out. Train it in calm moments this week so it's automatic when you actually need it — skills you only practice in emergencies don't show up in emergencies.

**Habit:** Use the reset breath once at practice this week — when tired, frustrated, or nervous.
**Reflect (littles, emoji):** How did dragon breathing feel?
**Reflect:** When did you deploy it this week, and what changed in the ten seconds after?

## Week 8 — Routines Beat Nerves

**Core:** Watch elite athletes before they compete: the same warm-up, the same music, the same sequence, every single time. That's not superstition — it's engineering. Nerves feed on uncertainty, and a routine deletes uncertainty: your body walks a familiar path, so your mind reads *I've been here before*, even somewhere brand new. This week you build yours. Keep it short — five to ten minutes — and make every piece portable: movement to wake your body, your reset breath, maybe one phrase you say to yourself. It only works if you run it every time, so practice it at... practice.

**Littles:** Make your own get-ready recipe! Maybe: jumping jacks, dragon breaths, touch your toes, say "let's go" to yourself. Same steps, same order, every practice. When your body knows the steps, the butterflies calm down — they've seen this before!

**Teens extra:** Design constraint: nothing in your routine can depend on the venue, the schedule, or other people — those are list-two items, and a routine built on them breaks exactly when you need it. Movement, breath, self-talk cue: all list one, all portable. Write yours as an actual checklist this week, then run it until it's boring. Boring is the goal — boring means automatic.

**Habit:** Design your pre-practice routine and run it at every practice this week.
**Reflect (littles, one word):** What's your favorite part of your get-ready recipe?
**Reflect:** Write your routine as a checklist. What did you notice by the third time running it?

## Week 9 — Your Process Goals

**Core:** There are two kinds of goals. Outcome goals name results: win the bracket, make the podium. Process goals name actions: attack first in every match, reset my breathing between periods, keep my stance low in the third. Here's the problem with outcome goals — they live on list two. You can do everything right and still lose to a great opponent. Process goals live entirely on list one: you can hit them in a loss and miss them in a win, which makes them the only honest measure of how *you* competed. This week, before the season starts, you choose yours. One to three process goals. Actions, not results. Yours completely.

**Littles:** A process goal is a DOING goal, not a winning goal. "Try my hardest the whole time." "Use my dragon breath when I get flustered." "Attack first." You can win your doing-goals even on a day you lose the match — because doing-goals are always up to YOU.

**Teens extra:** Make them specific enough to audit: "wrestle hard" is a wish; "initiate the first tie-up in every period" is a process goal — after the match, you know cleanly whether you did it. This is also how the debrief works from now on: you review your process goals, because they're the only thing that was ever yours to control. Set 1–3 for the season now. You'll adjust them at mid-season.

**Habit:** Write your 1–3 process goals for the season. Keep them where you'll see them.
**Reflect (littles, one word):** What's your doing-goal for this season?
**Reflect:** Your season's process goals — and for each: how, exactly, will you know after a match whether you hit it?
