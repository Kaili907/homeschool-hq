## The Last Bus from Harbor Road
**ID:** `g7-text-01`  
**Genre:** realistic fiction

At 6:12, the last bus waited beside the closed bait shop. Lena could see its interior lights through the fog. She could also see Mr. Bell kneeling beside a bicycle with a loose chain.

“Go,” he said. “I can walk it.”

The next bus would not come until morning. Lena had promised to be home by seven, and her phone battery showed two percent. She looked from the driver’s raised hand to the bicycle. The rear wheel was jammed against the frame. It would not roll.

Lena ran to the bus and asked the driver to wait one minute. The driver pointed to the schedule. “One minute,” she agreed, “not five.” Lena returned, turned the bicycle upside down, and remembered the repair demonstration from the community workshop. She pulled the derailleur arm forward and lifted the chain onto the smallest gear. The wheel turned.

Mr. Bell began to thank her, but Lena was already running. The bus door closed behind her at 6:14. The decision had cost two minutes and a streak of grease across her sleeve. It also changed the way she understood a schedule. A deadline could be firm without making every other responsibility disappear.

## What Makes a Source Trustworthy?
**ID:** `g7-text-02`  
**Genre:** informational

A trustworthy source is not simply a source that agrees with us or appears professional. Readers must examine how a claim was produced. Who created the information? What evidence is offered? Can the evidence be checked? Does the author have relevant expertise, direct access, or a reason to persuade the audience?

No single test is perfect. A scientist may have expertise but still make an error. An eyewitness may have direct experience but see only one part of an event. A government report may provide useful data while reflecting the categories officials chose to measure. A popular video may explain an idea clearly but omit uncertainty.

Strong researchers use several moves together. They trace a claim to its original source, read beyond a headline, compare independent reporting, examine dates, distinguish evidence from opinion, and look for corrections. They also ask what information is missing. Trust is not a permanent label placed on a website. It is a judgment supported by transparent checks—and it should change when better evidence appears.

## The Geometry of a Skate Park
**ID:** `g7-text-03`  
**Genre:** informational

A skate park looks like a collection of ramps and rails, but its design depends on connected geometric decisions. A ramp that rises too sharply may be unsafe for beginners. A curve that changes suddenly can interrupt motion. Designers use scale drawings to test dimensions before concrete is poured.

Suppose a drawing uses one centimeter to represent half a meter. A platform shown as six centimeters long will be three meters long in the park. The scale must be applied to every dimension, including widths, heights, and spaces between features. Designers also calculate areas to estimate surface materials and examine angles where ramps meet flat ground.

Geometry alone does not decide the final design. Builders must consider drainage, visibility, accessibility, cost, skill levels, and how people move through shared space. The best plan is not the one with the most complicated shapes. It is the one that uses measurement and evidence to create a safe, durable, and welcoming place.

## Letter from a Future Archaeologist
**ID:** `g7-text-04`  
**Genre:** speculative informational fiction

To the research team: We recovered twenty-seven objects from the sealed room, including three cracked screens, a metal bottle, six identical cables, and a stack of paper cards marked with dates. We should resist the temptation to name the room too quickly.

One hypothesis is that it served as a classroom. The dated cards may have organized learning tasks, and the screens may have displayed information. A second hypothesis is that it was a meeting room used by adults. The bottle and cables do not distinguish between these possibilities.

We need context. Are similar cards found elsewhere in the building? Do the screen heights suggest seated users? Are there signs of food preparation, storage, or specialized tools? An object rarely explains itself. Its meaning depends on location, association, wear, and comparison.

Our report should separate observation from inference. “Six cables were found beside the screens” is an observation. “The cables were used daily by students” is an inference. Future evidence may support that idea—or replace it with a better one.

## Should a School Garden Sell Produce?
**ID:** `g7-text-05`  
**Genre:** argument

Selling a small portion of school-garden produce could teach budgeting, communication, and entrepreneurship. Revenue might purchase seeds, tools, or accessibility improvements. Students could calculate costs, set transparent prices, and report how funds were used.

However, a market can shift attention away from learning and shared access. Produce may not grow evenly, and students should not be pressured to perform unpaid labor. Food-safety rules, permissions, transportation, and handling of money would require adult oversight. Families should not feel that participation depends on buying something.

A reasonable pilot would begin with clear limits. Participation would be optional, adults would handle legal and food-safety requirements, and some produce would remain available through a free-sharing system. The class would define the learning goals before choosing what to sell. Success would be measured not only by revenue, but also by safety, fairness, accurate records, and what students learned from revising the plan.

## Fault Lines
**ID:** `g7-text-06`  
**Genre:** poetry

The sidewalk keeps a thin black seam
where winter lifted stone from stone.
Ants cross it like a border town;
small roots keep widening it below.

A map may draw one steady line,
but pressure gathers out of sight.
The ground remembers every push,
then shifts a fraction in the night.

I think of words we did not say,
of silence pressed beneath the floor.
A crack is not the whole collapse—
it may be where we start repair.
