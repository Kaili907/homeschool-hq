## The Library Seed Exchange
**ID:** `g5-text-01`  
**Genre:** realistic fiction

Every spring, the small library on Cedar Street placed a wooden drawer beside the return desk. The label read SEEDS TO SHARE. Neighbors left envelopes of bean, sunflower, herb, and tomato seeds. Anyone could take a few, as long as they promised to return seeds after the harvest.

Mina wanted to join, but the balcony outside her apartment received only a narrow stripe of sunlight. She almost put the bean envelope back. Then Mr. Ortiz, the librarian, helped her compare the growing notes on several packets. Mint could handle partial shade, but its roots spread quickly. A small tomato needed more light. Lettuce might grow in a shallow box and could be harvested a little at a time.

Mina chose lettuce. She measured the sunny stripe each hour for one Saturday and moved the box to follow it. Some seedlings bent toward the window, so she turned the box every morning. By June, the leaves were not enormous, but there were enough for several sandwiches. Mina let one plant flower and form seeds. In September, she returned a carefully labeled envelope to the drawer. On the back she wrote, “Good for small spaces. Turn the box toward the light.” Her harvest had become advice for the next gardener.

## Why City Trees Matter
**ID:** `g5-text-02`  
**Genre:** informational

A city tree does more than make a street look pleasant. Its leaves cast shade on sidewalks, buildings, and parked cars. Shaded surfaces usually heat more slowly than surfaces in direct sun. A row of healthy trees can therefore make a walking route more comfortable during hot weather.

Trees also interact with water. Rain lands on leaves and branches before it reaches the ground. Some water evaporates, while some drips more slowly into soil. Roots create spaces in the ground where water can move. These effects do not prevent every flood, but they can reduce the speed at which storm water reaches drains.

City trees provide habitat as well. Birds may nest in branches, insects feed on leaves or nectar, and small animals use connected patches of vegetation as travel routes. People benefit from these living systems, but trees require care. Young trees need water, damaged branches must be inspected, and the right species must be planted in the right space. A large tree beneath power lines can create future problems. Good city planning treats a tree as long-term infrastructure: something living that provides services when people protect it wisely.

## The Map in the Attic
**ID:** `g5-text-03`  
**Genre:** mystery fiction

The map was folded inside a recipe box, between cards for corn bread and apple cake. At first, Theo thought the blue lines showed roads. Then he noticed tiny fish drawn beside one line and a row of willow trees beside another.

“Maybe it is the creek before the new houses were built,” Grandma said.

Theo carried the map to the back window. The neighborhood street curved in the same shape as the wide blue line. At the bottom of the hill, a storm drain rattled after the rain. Theo compared the map with a current map on the town website. The old creek was missing, but the low ground still formed a winding path through yards and parks.

The next day, Theo and Grandma walked the route. They did not enter private property. From sidewalks and public paths, they found damp soil, willow trees, and grates where water disappeared underground. The mystery was not a hidden treasure. It was a hidden waterway. Theo scanned the map and wrote down where it came from so the library could preserve a copy. “A map can show what is gone,” he wrote, “but the land may still remember.”

## A Drop’s Long Trip
**ID:** `g5-text-04`  
**Genre:** science explanation

Imagine a drop of water resting in Lake Superior. Sunlight transfers energy to the surface, and some liquid water becomes water vapor. Air currents carry the vapor, which cools and condenses into tiny droplets in a cloud. When the droplets grow heavy enough, they may fall as rain or snow.

The drop’s next path depends on the land. It might soak into soil and move slowly underground. Plant roots could absorb it, and the plant might later release water vapor from its leaves. The drop might run over rock into a stream, travel through a river, and return to a lake. It might freeze for a season or remain underground for years.

People sometimes draw the water cycle as a neat circle, but real water follows many branching paths. Gravity pulls water downhill, sunlight provides energy for evaporation, and living things move water through ecosystems. The amount of water on Earth changes very little, yet the location and form of water change constantly. That is why protecting one part of a watershed can affect many places downstream.

## Two Plans for the Playground
**ID:** `g5-text-05`  
**Genre:** paired argument

PLAN A: Keep the center field open. An open field can support soccer, tag, relay games, community events, and unstructured play. Painted boundary marks could create several temporary game areas without adding large equipment. Supporters argue that flexible space serves many ages and costs less to maintain.

PLAN B: Add shaded activity zones. The field is useful, but on hot or rainy days it may be uncomfortable. Supporters propose trees, a shade structure, accessible tables, and a quiet sensory area around the edges. They argue that the playground should serve people who do not want or cannot join running games.

Both plans solve real problems. The town has enough money to begin only one major change this year. A strong recommendation would compare cost, number of users, safety, maintenance, accessibility, and whether parts of the other plan could be added later. The question is not which group matters. The question is how evidence can guide a fair sequence of improvements.

## Lanterns After Rain
**ID:** `g5-text-06`  
**Genre:** poetry

Puddles hold the evening sky,
small windows laid along the street.
Bicycle tires stitch silver lines;
wet branches tap a quiet beat.

One porch lamp wakes, then another—
lanterns answering through the gray.
The storm has carried dust from roofs,
and left the whole block washed with day.

I step around one mirrored cloud,
then stop before my shoe comes down.
For half a breath, the moon is there,
floating upside down in town.
