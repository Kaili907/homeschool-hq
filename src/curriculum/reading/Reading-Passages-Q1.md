## g4-01 · How Rainbows Form
grade: 4
words: 142

Have you ever wondered how a rainbow appears in the sky? A rainbow forms when sunlight passes through tiny drops of water in the air. This usually happens right after it rains, when the sun begins to shine again. Sunlight looks white, but it is really made of many colors mixed together. When light enters a raindrop, the drop bends the light and splits it apart. Each color bends by a slightly different amount, so the colors spread out into a band. That is why we see red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and purple in an arc. To spot a rainbow, stand with the sun behind you and look toward the falling rain. Rainbows are always there when the conditions are right, even if we cannot always see them. They remind us that light is full of hidden colors waiting to appear.

Once the wings are strong, the butterfly flies away to find flowers. This journey from egg to butterfly is called metamorphosis, and it is one of nature's greatest surprises.

## g4-03 · Why Leaves Change Color
grade: 4
words: 140

In the fall, many trees put on a colorful show. Their green leaves slowly turn to bright shades of yellow, orange, and red. But why does this happen? During spring and summer, leaves are full of a green material called chlorophyll. This material helps the tree make food from sunlight. The green color is so strong that it hides the other colors in the leaf. When autumn comes, the days grow shorter and the air turns cooler. The tree senses these changes and stops making chlorophyll. As the green fades away, the yellow and orange colors that were always there begin to show. Some trees also make new red colors in their leaves. After a while, the leaves dry out and fall to the ground. The tree rests through the winter and grows fresh green leaves again in the spring.

## g4-04 · The Water Cycle
grade: 4
words: 138

Water is always moving around our planet in a journey called the water cycle. It starts when the sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers. Some of this water turns into an invisible gas called water vapor and rises into the sky. This process is known as evaporation. High in the air, the vapor cools down and forms tiny droplets that gather into clouds. When the clouds hold too much water, the droplets fall back to the ground as rain or snow. This is called precipitation. The water that lands may soak into the soil or flow into streams and rivers. In time, much of it travels back to the ocean. Then the whole cycle begins all over again. The water cycle never stops, and it gives every living thing the fresh water it needs to survive.

## g6-01 · The Wonders of Coral Reefs
grade: 6
words: 221

Beneath the surface of warm, shallow oceans lies one of the most colorful and important habitats on the entire planet: the coral reef. Although a reef may look like a collection of strange rocks and plants, it is actually built by millions of tiny living animals called coral polyps. Each polyp is smaller than your fingernail, yet together these creatures create enormous structures over thousands of years. As older polyps die, they leave behind a hard, stony skeleton, and new polyps grow on top of them. Slowly, layer by layer, a massive reef takes shape. Coral reefs are sometimes called the rainforests of the sea because so many different creatures depend on them. Fish of every color dart among the coral branches, while crabs, sea turtles, eels, and octopuses find food and shelter there. Scientists believe that a quarter of all ocean species rely on reefs at some point in their lives. Unfortunately, coral reefs are quite fragile and can be harmed by pollution, warming water, and careless visitors. When the water grows too warm, corals may turn pale and weak in a process called bleaching. People all around the world are now working to protect these underwater treasures. By keeping the oceans clean and healthy, we can make sure that coral reefs continue to thrive for many generations to come.

## g6-02 · Understanding Volcanoes
grade: 6
words: 212

A volcano is one of the most powerful and fascinating features found anywhere on Earth. Deep beneath the ground, our planet is extremely hot, and in some places the rock becomes so hot that it melts into a thick, glowing liquid called magma. Because magma is lighter than the solid rock around it, it slowly rises toward the surface, collecting in large underground chambers. When the pressure grows too great, the magma bursts upward through cracks and openings, and a volcano erupts. Once magma reaches the surface and flows out, it is given a new name: lava. Lava can be incredibly hot, sometimes hotter than a thousand degrees. As it cools and hardens, it forms new layers of rock. Over many eruptions across thousands of years, these layers can build up into towering mountains. Not all volcanoes behave the same way. Some erupt gently, letting lava ooze out slowly, while others explode with tremendous force, sending ash and rock high into the sky. Scientists who study volcanoes are called volcanologists, and they work hard to understand the warning signs before an eruption. Their research helps keep nearby communities safe. Although volcanoes can be dangerous, they also create rich soil and new land, reminding us that our planet is always changing and alive.

## g6-03 · The Journey of Migrating Birds
grade: 6

Rather than struggle through a harsh winter, they take to the sky and travel to warmer regions where insects, seeds, and berries are still plentiful. Some birds fly only a short distance, but others cross entire continents and vast oceans. The tiny arctic tern, for example, travels from the top of the world to the bottom and back again, covering an astonishing distance each year. How do birds find their way across such enormous distances without maps or signs? Scientists believe they use a combination of clever tools. Many birds follow the position of the sun and stars, while others appear to sense the invisible magnetic field of the Earth. Some even recognize rivers, coastlines, and mountains far below them. Migration is a difficult and tiring journey, and birds must build up fat before they leave to fuel their long flight. When spring returns, these determined travelers make the trip once more, returning home to raise their young in familiar places.

## g6-04 · How Plants Make Their Food
grade: 6
words: 222

Unlike animals, plants cannot move around to search for a meal, so they have developed a truly remarkable way of feeding themselves. Through a process called photosynthesis, plants are able to make their own food using nothing more than sunlight, water, and air. This process takes place mainly inside the leaves, which act like small, natural factories. Deep within the leaves are tiny structures that contain a green substance called chlorophyll. It is this chlorophyll that gives most plants their familiar green color, and it plays a starring role in capturing energy from the sun. To make food, a plant draws water up from the soil through its roots and pulls in a gas called carbon dioxide from the air through tiny holes in its leaves. Using the energy trapped from sunlight, the plant combines these ingredients to create a kind of sugar, which serves as its food. As a wonderful bonus, the plant releases oxygen into the air as it works. This oxygen is the very same gas that people and animals need to breathe. In this way, plants quietly support nearly all life on Earth. Without photosynthesis, there would be no food for plant eaters, and the air itself would be very different. The next time you pass a leafy tree, remember the amazing work happening inside every green leaf.

## g6-05 · The Amazing Human Brain
grade: 6
words: 216

Inside your head sits one of the most complex and astonishing objects in the known universe: the human brain. Weighing only about three pounds, this soft, wrinkled organ controls almost everything you do, think, and feel. Every time you move a muscle, solve a puzzle, remember a friend's name, or laugh at a joke, your brain is hard at work. The brain is made up of billions of tiny cells called neurons, which send messages to one another using quick bursts of electricity and chemicals. These messages travel along pathways at incredible speeds, allowing you to react to the world almost instantly. Different parts of the brain handle different jobs. One area helps you see, another lets you understand language, and still another controls your balance so you can walk and run without falling. Perhaps the most amazing thing about the brain is its ability to learn and change. Each time you practice a new skill, such as playing an instrument or riding a bicycle, your brain forms stronger connections between its cells. This means that the more you practice, the easier a task becomes. Scientists are still discovering new secrets about how the brain works. ## g6-06 · Life in the Desert
grade: 6
words: 221

At first glance, a desert may appear to be an empty and lifeless place, filled with nothing but sand, rock, and endless sky. In truth, deserts are home to a surprising variety of plants and animals that have found clever ways to survive in one of the harshest environments on Earth. A desert is defined not by heat but by how little rain it receives, and many deserts go for months, or even years, without a single storm. The creatures that live there must be experts at saving water. The camel, often called the ship of the desert, can travel for long stretches without drinking, storing energy as fat in its large hump. Many small animals, such as mice and lizards, avoid the blazing daytime heat by hiding underground in cool burrows and coming out only at night. Desert plants have their own tricks as well. The cactus, for instance, stores water inside its thick stem and protects itself with sharp spines. Its waxy skin helps keep precious moisture from escaping into the dry air. When rain finally does arrive, the desert can transform almost overnight, as hidden seeds burst into bloom and cover the ground with color. These bursts of life remind us that even the driest and most challenging places on our planet are full of wonder and surprise.

## g6-07 · The Story of Recycling
grade: 6
words: 222

Every day, people around the world throw away enormous amounts of trash, and much of it does not simply disappear. Instead, it can pile up in landfills or pollute our rivers, forests, and oceans for a very long time. Fortunately, there is a helpful solution that anyone can take part in, and it is called recycling. Recycling is the process of taking used materials and turning them into brand new products, rather than throwing them away. Many everyday items can be recycled, including paper, cardboard, glass, metal cans, and certain kinds of plastic. When these materials are collected, they are carried to special centers where they are sorted, cleaned, and broken down. From there, they can be melted, shredded, or pressed and then used to make fresh goods. A single glass bottle, for example, can be recycled again and again without ever losing its quality. Recycling brings many important benefits to our planet. It saves natural resources like trees, water, and oil, and it uses far less energy than making products from scratch. It also reduces the amount of waste that ends up buried in the ground. By sorting our trash carefully and choosing to reuse whenever we can, each of us can help protect the environment. Small actions, repeated by millions of people, can add up to a very big difference.

## g6-08 · Exploring the Night Sky

They appear small only because they are unimaginably far away, so distant that their light travels for many years before it reaches our eyes. ## g4-11 · Why the Sky Isn't Blue
grade: 4
words: 144

The sky looks blue, but the air itself has no color. What we see is sunlight being scattered.

Sunlight looks white, but it contains every color mixed together. When that light hits the gases in our atmosphere, the colors do not scatter equally. Blue light travels in shorter, tighter waves, and short waves bounce off air molecules much more easily than long ones. So blue light gets thrown in every direction across the whole sky, while red and orange light mostly travels straight through.

That also explains sunsets. When the sun sits low, its light must pass through far more air to reach your eyes. By then, most of the blue has scattered away, leaving the long red and orange waves to come through.

So the sky is not blue, exactly. The sky is just very good at bouncing blue light at your eyes.

## g4-14 · Left-Handed Advantage

## g4-16 · The Hundred-Year Nap
grade: 4
words: 140

Some seeds can wait a very long time.

In 2005, scientists successfully sprouted a date palm seed found at an ancient desert fortress in Israel. Carbon dating placed the seed at roughly two thousand years old. It grew into a healthy tree.

Seeds survive by shutting down almost completely. Moisture is removed, chemical activity slows nearly to a stop, and a hard coat seals everything inside. In that state, a seed is not really growing or dying — it is waiting for the right combination of water, warmth, and time.

## g6-09 · The Problem With Averages
grade: 6
words: 212

A statistician once drowned crossing a river with an average depth of three feet. The joke is old, but the lesson underneath it is worth keeping.

An average compresses many numbers into one, and in doing so it deliberately throws away information. That is useful when the numbers cluster tightly — the average height of a sixth-grade class tells you something real. It becomes misleading when the numbers spread widely, or when a few extreme values pull the number away from anything typical.

Consider ten workers. Nine earn thirty thousand dollars a year; one earns three million. The average salary at that company is roughly three hundred twenty-seven thousand dollars — a figure that describes nobody who works there.

This is why analysts also report the median, the middle value when everything is lined up in order. In our example, the median salary is thirty thousand: an honest picture of a typical worker.

Neither number is wrong. They answer different questions. The average asks, "If we pooled everything and split it evenly, what would each share be?" The median asks, "What is the experience of someone in the middle?"

When you read a statistic in the news, it is worth asking which question was answered — and which one you actually wanted.

Engineers do not try to build bridges that cannot move. They build bridges that move correctly.

A structure that resists all force must absorb that force somewhere, and materials under enough stress eventually crack. A structure that flexes converts some of that force into motion, spreading the load across time instead of concentrating it in one moment. This is why tall buildings are designed to sway in high wind and why suspension bridges rise and fall by measurable amounts as traffic crosses.

The famous failure of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in 1940 was not caused by flexibility alone. It was caused by resonance — wind striking the deck at a rhythm that matched the bridge's own natural frequency, so each gust added energy to a motion already underway, like a child pumping their legs on a swing. The oscillations grew until the deck tore itself apart.

Modern designs interrupt that feedback. Open gratings let wind pass through rather than pushing against a solid face. Dampers absorb energy before it accumulates. Deck shapes are tested in wind tunnels specifically to avoid frequencies that could build.

The goal was never stillness. The goal is motion that stays under control.

## g6-11 · The Cost of Free
grade: 6
words: 190

When an app costs nothing, the money still comes from somewhere.

Most free platforms operate on advertising, which means their actual customers are advertisers, not users. What the platform sells is access to your attention, and increasingly, information about you that makes predicting your behavior more accurate.

This shapes design in ways that are easy to miss. A feature that makes you close the app satisfied is, from a revenue standpoint, worse than a feature that keeps you scrolling while dissatisfied. Endless feeds, autoplay, streak counters, and notifications are not accidents — they are engineering aimed at a measurable target called engagement.

None of this requires anyone to be a villain. Companies respond to incentives, and the incentive is time spent.

The useful habit is not paranoia but arithmetic. Ask: who pays for this, and what do they get? A paid app answers simply — you pay, they build something you want to keep using. A free app has a more complicated answer, and the answer is usually you.

Understanding that trade does not mean refusing it. It means choosing it deliberately, with the price tag visible instead of hidden.

## g6-12 · Fermentation
grade: 6
words: 169

Long before anyone understood microbiology, humans figured out that food left alone under the right conditions sometimes became better instead of worse.

Fermentation is controlled decay. Bacteria or yeast consume sugars in food and release acids, alcohol, or gases as byproducts. Those byproducts change flavor and texture — and, crucially, create an environment hostile to the organisms that cause dangerous spoilage. Sauerkraut keeps through a winter because the lactic acid produced by fermentation makes the cabbage inhospitable to almost everything else.

Nearly every culture developed its own version independently: kimchi in Korea, miso and natto in Japan, yogurt across Central Asia, cheese throughout Europe, pickles nearly everywhere. Each solved the same problem — how do you make food survive a season — using whatever microorganisms lived locally.

The remarkable part is that this worked for thousands of years without anyone knowing why. Louis Pasteur did not identify microorganisms as the mechanism until the 1850s.

Humans had been successfully managing invisible life for millennia. They simply called it a recipe.

## g6-13 · The Practice Paradox
grade: 6
words: 172

Athletes and musicians who improve fastest tend to enjoy their practice sessions least — and this is not a coincidence.

Research on expert performance distinguishes ordinary repetition from deliberate practice. Ordinary repetition means running through what you already do well. It feels productive, it feels smooth, and it produces very little improvement. Deliberate practice means working specifically at the edge of your ability, where mistakes are frequent and progress is uncomfortable.

The discomfort is the mechanism. Errors are information; a session without them is a session that generated no data about what needs fixing. This is why a musician might spend forty minutes on four difficult measures instead of playing the whole piece, and why a wrestler drills the position they are worst in rather than the throw they already own.

The paradox is that our instincts point the wrong direction. We gravitate toward what feels good, and what feels good is competence.

Improvement lives in the other place — the awkward, clumsy, slightly embarrassing edge where you are not good yet.

## g6-14 · Reading the Weather Without a Phone
grade: 6
words: 169

Before satellites, people forecast weather by reading pressure, wind, and clouds — and the underlying logic still works.

Falling air pressure generally signals approaching storms, because low-pressure systems draw air upward, where it cools and condenses into cloud and rain. Rising pressure suggests settling weather. Old barometers were valued precisely because they made this invisible change readable.

Cloud shape carries information too. High wispy cirrus clouds, made of ice crystals far above the ground, often arrive a day or so ahead of a warm front. Towering cumulonimbus clouds with flat anvil tops indicate powerful vertical air movement — the engine behind thunderstorms.

Even the old saying about red sky at night has physics behind it in the middle latitudes, where weather generally travels west to east. A red sunset means light is passing through dry, dust-laden air to the west, which is the air heading your way.

None of this beats a modern forecast. But it explains where forecasts come from, and it works when the signal does not.

## g6-15 · The Committee in Your Head
grade: 6
words: 173

People often speak of "making up your mind" as if the mind were a single thing that decides. Research suggests something closer to a committee.

Different neural systems evaluate a choice using different criteria and on different timescales. One system responds quickly to immediate reward. Another weighs long-term consequences more heavily but works slower and tires more easily. When these systems disagree, we experience it as internal conflict — wanting to stop scrolling and continuing to scroll, intending to start homework and not starting.

Understanding this changes the practical strategy. Willpower framed as a battle between "you" and "temptation" tends to fail, because the slower system loses when it is tired, hungry, or overloaded.

What works better is design. Removing a phone from the room, laying out clothes the night before, or committing publicly to a plan shifts the decision to a moment when the deliberate system is strong, so the impulsive system faces fewer opportunities later.

You are not overpowering yourself. You are arranging conditions so that fewer arguments happen at all.

## g6-16 · Maps Lie
grade: 6
words: 187

Every flat map of Earth is wrong, and it has to be.

The planet is roughly a sphere, and a sphere's surface cannot be flattened without stretching, tearing, or compressing something. Cartographers call the workarounds projections, and each one chooses which distortion to accept.

The Mercator projection, familiar from classrooms and navigation apps, preserves angles — which made it invaluable for sailors, since a straight line on the map corresponds to a constant compass bearing. The cost is area. Landmasses far from the equator appear vastly larger than they are. On a Mercator map, Greenland looks comparable to Africa. In reality, Africa is roughly fourteen times larger.

Other projections make other trades. Equal-area projections preserve relative size but distort shape, sometimes dramatically. Compromise projections distribute error more evenly and preserve nothing perfectly.

There is no honest map without a choice, and the choice reveals a purpose. A map for navigation, a map for comparing nations, and a map for showing climate zones will legitimately disagree about what Earth looks like.

The question is never whether a map is accurate. It is what the mapmaker decided to sacrifice.
